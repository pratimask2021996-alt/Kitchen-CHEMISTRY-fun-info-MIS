Try it yourself

Five experiments that use things already in most kitchens. A couple of ground rules first: get an adult's okay before you start, keep one nearby for anything involving the stove, and treat everything here as food-grade curiosity — not a recipe to eat afterwards.

Red Cabbage pH Rainbow

15 minutes · adult help optional

You'll need

  • A few red cabbage leaves
  • Boiling water
  • Several clear cups
  • Lemon juice, vinegar, a baking soda solution, soapy water

Steps

  • Chop the cabbage, pour boiling water over it, and let it steep 10 minutes until deep purple.
  • Strain out the leaves — the purple liquid is your indicator.
  • Pour a little into each cup, then add a different kitchen liquid to each.
  • Watch the colour shift in real time.

The science: red cabbage contains anthocyanin pigments that change colour with pH — pink in acids, purple when neutral, blue-green in bases. It's the same chemistry behind commercial pH strips.

Kitchen Volcano

5 minutes · great for younger cooks

You'll need

  • Baking soda
  • Vinegar
  • A tall bottle or cup, on a tray
  • Food colouring (optional)

Steps

  • Add a few spoonfuls of baking soda to the bottle.
  • Colour some vinegar with a drop of food colouring.
  • Pour the vinegar in and step back.

The science: vinegar (acetic acid) reacts with baking soda (a base) to produce carbon dioxide, water, and sodium acetate — the same gas-releasing reaction that leavens a cake, just faster and far more dramatic.

Homemade Paneer

20 minutes · adult supervision for the stove

You'll need

  • Full-fat milk
  • A pan
  • Lemon juice or vinegar
  • A strainer and thin cloth

Steps

  • Heat the milk until it just starts to simmer.
  • Turn off the heat and stir in a tablespoon of lemon juice or vinegar.
  • Watch it separate into curds and thin, yellowish whey within a minute.
  • Strain through the cloth to collect the curds.

The science: milk proteins (casein) normally repel each other and stay suspended. Acid neutralises that charge, so the proteins clump together and coagulate — the same principle behind cheese-making everywhere.

Liquid Density Tower

10 minutes · no heat needed

You'll need

  • A tall clear glass
  • Honey, dish soap, coloured water, cooking oil
  • Rubbing alcohol (optional, coloured)

Steps

  • Pour honey into the glass first.
  • Slowly pour dish soap on top, down the side of the glass.
  • Add the coloured water the same way, then the oil, then the alcohol.
  • Watch the liquids settle into distinct layers.

The science: each liquid has a different density — mass packed into the same volume — so denser liquids sink below lighter ones as long as they don't mix. It's exactly why oil floats on vinegar in a salad dressing until you shake it.

Invisible Lemon Juice Ink

15 minutes · needs a warm lamp or iron

You'll need

  • Lemon juice
  • A cotton swab or thin brush
  • Plain paper
  • A warm lamp bulb, or an iron (adult use)

Steps

  • Dip the swab in lemon juice and write a message on the paper.
  • Let it dry completely — it should be invisible.
  • Hold the paper near a warm bulb, or have an adult run a warm iron over it.
  • Watch the message turn brown.

The science: lemon juice is made of weak organic acids that soak into the paper. Gentle heat oxidises and chars them before it chars the surrounding paper — a low-temperature cousin of caramelization.
