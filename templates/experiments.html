Try it yourself

Ten experiments that use things already in most kitchens. A couple of ground rules first: get an adult's okay before you start, keep one nearby for anything involving the stove, and treat everything here as food-grade curiosity — not a recipe to eat afterwards.

Red Cabbage pH Rainbow

15 minutes · adult help optional

You'll need

  • A few red cabbage leaves
  • Boiling water
  • Several clear cups
  • Lemon juice, vinegar, a baking soda solution, soapy water

Steps

  • Chop the cabbage, pour boiling water over it, and let it steep 10 minutes until deep purple.
  • Strain out the leaves — the purple liquid is your indicator.
  • Pour a little into each cup, then add a different kitchen liquid to each.
  • Watch the colour shift in real time.

The science: red cabbage contains anthocyanin pigments that change colour with pH — pink in acids, purple when neutral, blue-green in bases. It's the same chemistry behind commercial pH strips.

Kitchen Volcano

5 minutes · great for younger cooks

You'll need

  • Baking soda
  • Vinegar
  • A tall bottle or cup, on a tray
  • Food colouring (optional)

Steps

  • Add a few spoonfuls of baking soda to the bottle.
  • Colour some vinegar with a drop of food colouring.
  • Pour the vinegar in and step back.

The science: vinegar (acetic acid) reacts with baking soda (a base) to produce carbon dioxide, water, and sodium acetate — the same gas-releasing reaction that leavens a cake, just faster and far more dramatic.

Homemade Paneer

20 minutes · adult supervision for the stove

You'll need

  • Full-fat milk
  • A pan
  • Lemon juice or vinegar
  • A strainer and thin cloth

Steps

  • Heat the milk until it just starts to simmer.
  • Turn off the heat and stir in a tablespoon of lemon juice or vinegar.
  • Watch it separate into curds and thin, yellowish whey within a minute.
  • Strain through the cloth to collect the curds.

The science: milk proteins (casein) normally repel each other and stay suspended. Acid neutralises that charge, so the proteins clump together and coagulate — the same principle behind cheese-making everywhere.

Liquid Density Tower

10 minutes · no heat needed

You'll need

  • A tall clear glass
  • Honey, dish soap, coloured water, cooking oil
  • Rubbing alcohol (optional, coloured)

Steps

  • Pour honey into the glass first.
  • Slowly pour dish soap on top, down the side of the glass.
  • Add the coloured water the same way, then the oil, then the alcohol.
  • Watch the liquids settle into distinct layers.

The science: each liquid has a different density — mass packed into the same volume — so denser liquids sink below lighter ones as long as they don't mix. It's exactly why oil floats on vinegar in a salad dressing until you shake it.

Invisible Lemon Juice Ink

15 minutes · needs a warm lamp or iron

You'll need

  • Lemon juice
  • A cotton swab or thin brush
  • Plain paper
  • A warm lamp bulb, or an iron (adult use)

Steps

  • Dip the swab in lemon juice and write a message on the paper.
  • Let it dry completely — it should be invisible.
  • Hold the paper near a warm bulb, or have an adult run a warm iron over it.
  • Watch the message turn brown.

The science: lemon juice is made of weak organic acids that soak into the paper. Gentle heat oxidises and chars them before it chars the surrounding paper — a low-temperature cousin of caramelization.

Rock Candy Crystals

20 min active + 5–7 days waiting

You'll need

  • Water and a lot of granulated sugar
  • A wooden skewer or clean string
  • A tall glass and a clothespin

Steps

  • Heat water to a simmer and dissolve in sugar, a little at a time, until no more will dissolve.
  • Let it cool slightly, then pour into the glass.
  • Suspend the skewer in the middle with the clothespin, touching neither the sides nor bottom.
  • Leave undisturbed for 5–7 days and watch crystals grow.

The science: the syrup holds more dissolved sugar than it normally could at room temperature — a supersaturated solution. As it cools and water evaporates, the extra sugar comes out of solution and locks onto the skewer as solid crystals.

The Naked Egg

5 min active + 24–48 hours waiting

You'll need

  • A raw egg
  • A jar
  • Enough vinegar to submerge it

Steps

  • Place the egg in the jar and cover completely with vinegar.
  • Watch bubbles form on the shell within minutes.
  • Leave for 24–48 hours, changing the vinegar once if it clouds up.
  • Gently rinse — the shell is gone, leaving a soft, translucent egg.

The science: eggshell is mostly calcium carbonate. Vinegar's acetic acid reacts with it, releasing carbon dioxide gas (the bubbles) and dissolving the shell away, leaving just the thin membrane that was underneath it.

Milk Plastic

20 minutes + a day to dry

You'll need

  • 1 cup milk
  • 4 tablespoons vinegar
  • A pan, strainer, and cloth
  • Cookie cutters (optional)

Steps

  • Heat the milk until just steaming, then stir in the vinegar.
  • Watch it curdle into soft white lumps within a minute.
  • Strain and rinse the curds, then knead like dough.
  • Press into a mould or cutter and air-dry for a day until firm.

The science: the same acid-triggered protein coagulation behind paneer produces casein curds that, once the whey is drained away, behave like a mouldable plastic — drying into a firm, lightweight solid.

Butter in a Jar

10–15 minutes of shaking

You'll need

  • Heavy cream, at room temperature
  • A jar with a tight lid
  • A marble (optional, speeds it up)

Steps

  • Fill the jar no more than halfway with cream.
  • Seal tightly and shake hard and continuously.
  • It turns to whipped cream, then suddenly clumps into butter, separating from a thin liquid.
  • Drain the liquid (that's buttermilk) and rinse the butter with cold water.

The science: shaking beats air into the cream's fat globules until they clump together so tightly that they force out the liquid trapped between them — the same overwhipping that turns whipped cream into butter.

Fizzy Lemonade

5 minutes · drinkable result

You'll need

  • Fresh lemon juice, sugar, water
  • A small pinch of baking soda
  • A tall glass

Steps

  • Stir lemon juice, sugar, and water together to taste.
  • Add a small pinch of baking soda and watch it fizz immediately.
  • Stir once more and drink while it's still fizzy.

The science: the citric acid in lemon juice reacts instantly with the baking soda, releasing carbon dioxide as tiny bubbles — a drinkable version of the same acid-base reaction that leavens a cake.
