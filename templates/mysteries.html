Kitchen mysteries, solved

The questions that come up while you're actually cooking. Tap one to reveal the answer.

Why do onions make you cry?+

Slicing an onion ruptures its cells and releases an enzyme that reacts with sulfur compounds stored inside, forming a volatile gas. It drifts up to your eyes, mixes with your tears, and forms a mild acid — which is what actually stings. Your eyes respond by producing more tears to flush it out.

Chilling the onion first slows the enzyme down, and cutting near a bowl of water or a fan helps carry the gas away before it reaches your eyes.

Why does bread dough rise?+

Yeast feeds on the sugars in flour and releases carbon dioxide gas as a byproduct. Gluten — a stretchy protein network formed when flour is mixed with water — traps that gas instead of letting it escape, so the dough inflates like thousands of tiny balloons.

Kneading develops more gluten, which is why a well-kneaded dough holds its rise better than one that's barely been worked.

Why does seared meat turn brown, but boiled meat doesn't?+

Browning needs the Maillard reaction, and Maillard needs temperatures above roughly 140°C — something a pot of boiling water can never reach, since water caps out at 100°C no matter how long it boils.

A dry pan or oven has no such ceiling, so a seared or roasted surface can climb well past that threshold and brown quickly, while a boiled or steamed piece of meat stays pale however long it cooks.

Why do cut apples and potatoes turn brown?+

Cutting exposes plant cells to oxygen, which triggers an enzyme called polyphenol oxidase. It reacts with compounds in the flesh to form brown pigments — a process called enzymatic browning, unrelated to heat.

Acid slows the enzyme down, which is why a splash of lemon juice keeps sliced apples pale, and keeping cut pieces underwater limits their oxygen exposure the same way.

Why does whipped cream turn into butter if you keep whisking?+

Whisking beats tiny air bubbles into cream, and the fat globules in the cream cluster around each bubble to hold it in place — that's whipped cream.

Keep whisking, and those fat globules start clumping together instead, squeezing out the liquid trapped between them and leaving behind a solid clump of fat: butter. It's the same fat, just reorganised.

Why does salt make food taste better — and keep it longer?+

Salt sharpens flavour by suppressing our perception of bitterness and making sweet and savoury notes more noticeable — food doesn't just taste "saltier," it tastes more like itself.

As a preservative, salt draws water out of bacterial and fungal cells through osmosis, dehydrating them faster than they can grow — the principle behind curing meat, pickling vegetables, and salting fish for storage.

Why does ice cream stay soft while a tray of ice turns rock hard?+

Plain water freezes into large, interlocking ice crystals — hard all the way through. Ice cream is churned while it freezes, which breaks up crystals before they can grow large.

It's also full of sugar, fat, and a little trapped air, all of which get in the way of crystal formation and lower the freezing point slightly. The result stays soft enough to scoop straight from the freezer.
