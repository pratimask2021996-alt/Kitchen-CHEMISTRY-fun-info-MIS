The science in your kitchen

Eight reactions explain almost everything that happens between your fridge and your dinner plate. Here's what's actually going on.

Browning & flavor

The Maillard Reaction

Sear a piece of meat, toast a slice of bread, or roast a coffee bean, and you're watching amino acids and sugars react under dry heat. Named after the French chemist Louis-Camille Maillard, the reaction breaks down proteins and carbohydrates and reassembles them into hundreds of new flavor and aroma compounds, along with that deep golden-brown colour.

It needs temperatures above roughly 140°C (285°F), which is why it barely happens in a pot of simmering water, capped at 100°C. Boil a chicken breast and it stays pale; sear it in a dry pan and it browns in minutes.

Pat food dry before searing — extra surface water has to boil off first, which steals heat and delays browning.

Sugar chemistry

Caramelization

Where Maillard needs protein, caramelization works on sugar alone. Heat sucrose past about 170°C (340°F) and its molecules break apart and recombine, releasing a cascade of nutty, buttery, faintly bitter compounds along with that familiar amber colour.

It's what turns granulated sugar into caramel sauce, and what gives slow-cooked onions their sweetness — though onions also pick up some Maillard browning from their small amount of protein, so the two reactions are usually working side by side.

Watch the pan closely once sugar starts to colour — the gap between perfect caramel and burnt sugar is often under a minute.

Oil meets water

Emulsification

Oil and water don't mix — until an emulsifier steps in. These are molecules with a water-loving head and an oil-loving tail, so they sit at the boundary between the two liquids and let one disperse as tiny droplets inside the other.

Egg yolk supplies the emulsifier in mayonnaise, a compound called lecithin; mustard does the job in a vinaigrette. Whisk hard enough and long enough, and thousands of microscopic oil droplets get trapped in the water, producing something that looks and behaves like a single smooth liquid.

An emulsion breaks when oil is added too fast, or the mixture is shocked by sudden heat or cold — add oil in a slow, steady stream while whisking.

Microbes at work

Fermentation

Fermentation hands the cooking over to microorganisms. Yeast eats the sugars in bread dough and releases carbon dioxide and a little ethanol as waste; the gas gets trapped in a stretchy gluten network and the dough rises. Bake it, and those same gas pockets set into the bread's crumb.

Lactic acid bacteria do a related job in yogurt, sourdough, and pickles — instead of gas, they produce lactic acid, which thickens milk into yogurt and gives fermented foods their characteristic tang.

A dough that's over-proofed collapses because the gluten network has stretched past its limit and can no longer hold the gas.

Gas & rise

Acids, Bases & Leavening

Baking soda is a base — sodium bicarbonate. Mixed with an acid — buttermilk, lemon juice, brown sugar, even cocoa powder — it reacts to release carbon dioxide gas on the spot, which leavens quick breads and pancakes.

Baking powder carries its own acid already mixed in with the soda, so it can leaven a batter with no acidic ingredients at all. Most baking powder is "double-acting": part of the gas releases the moment it gets wet, and the rest releases again once it hits oven heat.

Baking soda without enough acid to react with leaves a bitter, metallic aftertaste — that's the unreacted base you're tasting.

Heat & structure

Protein Denaturation

A raw egg white is mostly water and tightly folded proteins, which is why it's clear and runny. Heat — or a strong acid — breaks the weak bonds holding those proteins in their folded shape. They unfold, then re-bond with their neighbours into a new, tangled network that traps water and turns opaque and firm.

The same process firms up meat as it cooks, which is why doneness is really a protein-structure question — and why meat cooked past a certain point starts squeezing out moisture and turning dry, no matter how good the cut.

Ceviche "cooks" fish using only citrus acid, no heat required — acid denatures protein too.

How heat travels

Heat Transfer

Cooking methods differ mainly in how heat reaches the food. Conduction passes heat through direct contact — a hot pan against a steak — fast, but uneven, since only the touching surface heats first. Convection moves heat through a circulating fluid, like the hot air inside an oven or the currents in a pot of soup, cooking more evenly but more slowly.

Radiation delivers heat as electromagnetic waves — a grill's infrared glow, or a microwave exciting water molecules directly inside the food, which is why microwaved food heats from the inside out instead of the surface in.

A microwave doesn't brown food because it never gets hot and dry enough for Maillard or caramelization to happen.

Mixtures & texture

Solutions, Colloids & Gels

Not every kitchen mixture is the same kind of mixture. A solution is dissolved all the way down to individual molecules — sugar stirred into tea disappears completely. A colloid keeps its particles slightly larger and merely suspended rather than dissolved, like the fat droplets floating in milk or the air bubbles trapped in whipped cream.

A gel is a special case: a network of long protein or starch molecules — like the collagen in gelatin — that traps liquid inside itself as it cools, holding a shape without being fully solid. It's the difference between a sauce, a foam, and a panna cotta.

Shake a bottle of vinaigrette and it turns cloudy — a temporary colloid — then separates back into two layers as the emulsion breaks.
